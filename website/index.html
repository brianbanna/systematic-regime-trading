The Problem

In September 2008, the S&P 500 lost 30% in three weeks. Most systematic strategies were fully invested when the crash hit. By the time volatility indicators confirmed the regime change, the damage was done.

This project asks: can we detect regime shifts early enough to actually do something about them? And more importantly, can we do it better than just watching the VIX or a moving average?

The short answer: the 5-model ensemble detects turbulent regimes about 6 days before major drawdowns begin. During the 2008 crisis, the strategy lost 4.3% while the market lost 29%. But the honest answer is more nuanced, and the out-of-sample results tell a more complete story.

How It Works

190 NASDAQ Stocks → 6 Market Features → 5 Regime Models → Probability-Weighted Ensemble → Allocation Signal → Backtest vs SPY

Data: 190 NASDAQ stocks, daily OHLCV from 2000 to 2020. Cross-sectional features: market volatility, breadth, direction, correlation, ATR, volume, and VIX term structure.

Models: Five regime detectors, each capturing different aspects of market state:

  • HMM (5-state Gaussian, remapped to 3) captures hidden state dynamics and transition persistence
  • GARCH(1,1) with Student-t distribution models conditional volatility clustering
  • K-Means clusters on volatility and volume features
  • Gaussian Mixture Model provides proper Bayesian posterior probabilities (replacing K-Means' inverse-distance hack)
  • Markov-Switching (Hamilton 1989) estimates regime-dependent mean and variance of returns

Validation: Walk-forward with expanding window, 5-year minimum training, quarterly refit. 59 windows producing 3,771 strictly out-of-sample predictions. No model ever sees future data. SPY is the benchmark, not an equal-weight proxy.

Why do regimes persist? Three mechanisms: institutional investors rebalance on fixed schedules (monthly, quarterly) so they respond slowly to regime shifts. Volatility clusters due to leverage feedback loops, where falling prices force deleveraging which causes more selling. And retail panic is contagious, spreading through news cycles over days, not minutes. These frictions create the window that a regime model can exploit.

September 2008, Day by Day

On September 12, 2008, the ensemble's turbulent probability crossed 60%. Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy three days later. By September 19, the strategy had reduced its equity allocation from 85% to 12%. Over the next three months, the S&P 500 fell 29%. The strategy lost 4.3%.

This wasn't prediction. The model didn't know Lehman would fail. It detected a statistical pattern: cross-sectional volatility rising, market breadth collapsing, and inter-stock correlation spiking. The HMM and Markov-Switching models both flagged a regime transition. The GARCH conditional volatility exceeded its 67th percentile threshold. The GMM posterior shifted from 70% calm to 55% turbulent within a week.

The confirmation filter required just 1 day for defensive transitions (compared to 5 days for risk-on). By the time the broader market realized what was happening, the strategy was already mostly in cash.

Blue area: equity allocation. Red area: market drawdown. The strategy reduces exposure before and during every major drawdown.

Does the Model Actually Predict?

The most important question: when the model says "turbulent," does volatility actually increase? The answer is yes, and the separation is clear.

Realized volatility over the next 5, 10, and 20 days, grouped by the model's current regime prediction. Calm predictions correspond to ~12% annualized vol, turbulent to ~22%. The model is genuinely informative.

The median lead time is 6 days: the model typically flags a regime shift about a week before the drawdown accelerates. The information coefficient is small but positive (0.03 at 20-day horizon, t-stat 1.6), meaning the signal has modest but real predictive power.

The Hard Truth: 2021 to 2026

I ran the model forward on data it never saw. No refitting. No parameter changes. Just the models trained on 2000-2020, applied to 2021-2026.

The result: the regime strategies underperformed. Regime Momentum returned -4.1% annually (Sharpe -0.64) while buy-and-hold returned 9.4%. The simple SMA-200 crossover was the only strategy that worked out-of-sample, returning 9.7% with a Sharpe of 0.75.

In-sample vs out-of-sample Sharpe ratios. The regime models stayed too defensive during the post-COVID bull market. The trivial SMA-200 crossover was more robust.

Why it failed: The 2021-2025 market was unusual. After a sharp COVID crash, markets entered a sustained bull run with elevated volatility (due to Fed policy uncertainty, inflation, and rate hikes). The regime models interpreted this elevated vol as "turbulent" and stayed defensive, missing most of the recovery. The models need periodic recalibration to adapt to structural changes in the volatility regime.

What I'd change: Rolling recalibration instead of fixed models. Shorter training windows that weight recent data more heavily. Additional features like yield curve slope and credit spreads that capture the macro environment, not just equity cross-section statistics. And honestly, a simpler approach (like SMA-200 with vol overlay) might be more robust than a 5-model ensemble that's harder to adapt.

Extension: Crude Oil Regimes

To test whether the framework generalizes beyond equities, I applied the same HMM and GARCH models to WTI crude oil futures.

Crude oil price with regime classification. The model correctly identifies the 2008 crash, 2014-2016 oil glut, and the 2020 negative price event as turbulent regimes.

Cross-asset analysis shows a moderate positive correlation (0.25) between equity VIX and crude oil realized volatility. Commodity vol slightly leads equity vol at the 5-day horizon, suggesting that energy market stress can serve as an early warning for broader risk-off episodes. For a commodity trading desk, this framework could be extended with term structure features (contango vs backwardation), inventory data, and seasonal adjustments.

Model Diagnostics

Pairwise agreement rates between the five regime models. HMM and GMM agree most frequently (both use feature space), while GARCH and Markov-Switching (both return-based) form a second cluster. The diversity is what makes the ensemble work.

Fama-French factor regression shows an annualized alpha of 0.83% (t=0.59, p=0.56). Not statistically significant, which is expected for a risk management overlay rather than an alpha-generating strategy. The R-squared of 0.21 against the 5-factor model confirms that the strategy's return profile is genuinely different from standard factor exposures, making it useful as a portfolio diversifier.

What I Learned

The regime detection works. The trading doesn't. The models genuinely predict future volatility (12% vs 22% realized vol separation between calm and turbulent predictions). But converting that prediction into a profitable trading strategy is much harder than detecting the regime itself. The confirmation filter, execution lag, and position sizing decisions erode most of the theoretical edge.

Ensemble diversity matters more than individual model quality. Adding GMM and Markov-Switching improved the Sharpe from 0.52 to 0.74, not because they're better models individually, but because they disagree with the other three in useful ways. The ensemble reduces turnover and smooths out false positives.

Trivial benchmarks keep you honest. Before adding the SMA-200 and vol-managed benchmarks, the results looked impressive. After, it became clear that most of the "alpha" was just volatility scaling with extra steps. The marginal contribution of regime detection over simple vol management is about 0.27 Sharpe points, which is meaningful but modest.

Out-of-sample testing is everything. The in-sample Sharpe of 0.74 looked good. The out-of-sample Sharpe of -0.64 tells a different story. If I'd published only the in-sample results, I'd be misleading anyone who reads this. The failure mode (too defensive in a bull market) is just as informative as the success mode (protective during crashes).

If I were deploying this: I'd use it as a risk overlay, not a standalone strategy. Combine the regime signal with a base allocation determined by a portfolio optimizer. Recalibrate quarterly. Add macro features (yield curve, credit spreads, MOVE index) that the current model lacks. And honestly, start with the SMA-200 approach and add complexity only when you can prove it helps on truly out-of-sample data.
